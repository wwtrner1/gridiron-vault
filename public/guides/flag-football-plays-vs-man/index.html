Flag football coaching guide

4 flag football plays that beat man coverage

Mesh, stack releases, out-and-up and the choice route: how each one shakes a man defender, and how to keep it legal.

In man coverage each defender takes one receiver and follows him wherever he goes. It is aggressive and it takes away easy throws, but it has two weaknesses: a defender has to get around traffic to stay with his man, and he has to react to the receiver's break. Every man beater attacks one of those: crossing routes and stacked releases create traffic, and double moves and option routes punish a defender who reacts too early.

How to spot man before the snap

  • Each defender lines up across from one receiver, often closer than 5 yards and looking at him, not at the quarterback.
  • Send a receiver in motion. If a defender runs across the field with him, it's man.
  • One defender deep in the middle with everyone else locked on a receiver is Cover 1 (man with one safety). No one deep is all man, often with a blitz.

1. Mesh: crossers that create traffic

Why it works: The two crossers run past each other almost shoulder to shoulder. A defender trailing his man has to fight through the other receiver and the other defender, and one of them loses a step. Against zone, the crossers settle in the open window.

Coaching point: Receivers run their own routes and don't block anyone. Many leagues, including most flag leagues, flag a deliberate pick, so the rub has to come from the routes crossing, never from contact. Check your league's rulebook.

2. Stack release: nobody can press both

Why it works: Defenders lined up on a stack have to decide who takes whom before the snap. Whoever takes the back man has to work around the front man to stay with him, so the out underneath is open.

Coaching point: Line Y a yard behind Z so he isn't touched at the line. Z's job is to take his defender deep and stay out of Y's way.

3. Out-and-up: punish the jump

Why it works: A man defender who has seen the quick out drive on it. When he jumps the break, the receiver turns up behind him with no one deep on that side.

Coaching point: Call it after the quick out has worked once or twice. The quarterback pumps on the out to sell it, then throws the turn upfield.

4. Choice route: always break away

Why it works: A man defender has to pick a side to play. The choice route turns that against him by design: the receiver breaks to the side the defender gave up.

Coaching point: Spend practice time on it, because the receiver and quarterback must read it the same way. Start with a simple rule: break away from the defender's front foot.

Picking the right one

Against Cover 1 (man with a deep safety), stay underneath and across: Mesh, stack releases and choice routes beat their man and the safety can't help. Against all-out man (no deep help), take one shot: out-and-up or a single go route against a defender who's playing tight. Common questions

What is the best flag football play against man coverage?

Mesh (two crossing routes underneath) is the classic, because the crossers create traffic that man defenders have to fight through. Stack releases, choice routes and an out-and-up after a few quick outs also work.

Are pick plays legal in flag football?

Many flag leagues, including most youth and NFL FLAG leagues, penalize a deliberate pick or screen by a receiver. Crossing routes like Mesh are legal when receivers run their own routes without making contact. Check your league's rulebook.

How do you know if the defense is playing man or zone in flag football?

Send a receiver in motion before the snap. If a defender follows him across the formation, it's man; if the defenders just shift or stay put, it's zone.
